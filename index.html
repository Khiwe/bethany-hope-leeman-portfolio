Artist Statement

“Ways of seeing” — multimedia, lace on photograph, 2026

“Through sustained looking, I seek to challenge habits of consumption and encourage a slower, more relational way of seeing. Observation becomes an act of care.”

My practice explores care as a material, social, and political force. Working across painting, embroidery, crochet, and textile processes, I investigate the ways in which relationships are formed, sustained, and remembered through acts of making. I am interested in the quiet gestures that often go unnoticed, gestures of nurturing, mending, teaching, gathering, and tending, and how these acts shape our understanding of love, community, and belonging.

At the centre of my work is an ongoing exploration of womanhood and the histories embedded within domestic and handmade practices. I am drawn to materials traditionally associated with women's labour because they carry traces of intimacy, repetition, and care. Stitching, crocheting, and painting become methods through which I think about connection, inheritance, and the transmission of knowledge across generations. These processes allow me to slow down and pay attention to forms of labour that are often overlooked, yet essential to the ways we sustain one another.

My work is informed by bell hooks' understanding of love as a practice rather than a feeling. For hooks, love is an action grounded in care, responsibility, trust, and commitment. This conception of love has become foundational to my thinking, both within my life and within my artistic practice. I approach making as a form of attentiveness, asking how artistic processes can embody care and how art might create spaces for reflection, tenderness, and connection.

I am also deeply influenced by Christina Sharpe's writing on care, memory, and survival. Her work has shaped my understanding of how communities continue to create forms of support and kinship despite histories of violence, displacement, and loss. I am interested in the ways care emerges as a response to vulnerability, and how acts of making can become acts of remembrance, repair, and collective holding.

Alongside these influences, John Berger's Ways of Seeing has informed my relationship to images and representation. Berger reminds us that seeing is never neutral. The way we look at the world is shaped by history, culture, desire, and power. This understanding encourages me to consider not only what I represent, but how I look. My work often begins with close observation, particularly of flowers, landscapes, and everyday objects. Through sustained looking, I seek to challenge habits of consumption and encourage a slower, more relational way of seeing. Observation becomes an act of care.

Flowers frequently appear throughout my practice as both subject and metaphor. I am drawn to their associations with celebration, grief, beauty, remembrance, and transformation. Flowers accompany some of the most intimate moments of human experience. They exist within cycles of blooming and decay, reminding us of the fragile and temporary nature of life. Through floral imagery, I explore themes of growth, vulnerability, resilience, and interconnectedness.

As a South African artist, I am interested in the role that care plays within communities navigating complex social realities. I am inspired by the women, families, teachers, neighbours, and caregivers whose labour often remains invisible despite sustaining entire communities. My work seeks to honour these forms of care while imagining alternative ways of relating to one another through generosity, reciprocity, and collective responsibility.

Ultimately, I see my practice as an invitation to slow down and pay attention. Through painting and textile processes, I create spaces where care can be seen, felt, and valued. My work asks how we might cultivate more attentive relationships with one another, with our histories, and with the environments we inhabit. In a world increasingly defined by speed and disconnection, I am interested in art's capacity to nurture intimacy, foster community, and imagine love as a transformative force.

Selected Artworks

(02) • Intaglio Print & Oil Painting

ode to my mother

“We couldn't write, we didn't think to archive only to survive.”

An intaglio print and oil painting. Reflects on inheritance, survival, and feminine lineage through the image of the baobab tree, a symbol of endurance, shelter, and life within its ecosystem. The work honours my mother and the women before her whose strength and resilience created the conditions for my softness. Where they endured, protected, and survived, I have been given the freedom to become.

(03) • Photography & Mixed Media

a portrait of all the woman who came before me

“And you are living proof that we existed, that we were alive.”

The absence of familial archive and the instability of inherited identity. Through faceless female figures, distorted self-portraiture, and fictitious landscapes, the works speak to the disorientation of not knowing one's lineage or history. The contorted bodies and shifting environments become manifestations of a fragmented self — haunted by what cannot be traced, remembered, or recovered.

(04) • Charcoal Drawings to Oil Paintings

Return to the Garden

“Mothers and daughters existing as wretched mirrors of each other: i am all you could have been and you are all i might be.”

Return to the Garden and its different iterations from charcoal drawings to oil paintings — on the motherwound, ties to land, the garden, and an unknown history/heritage. Holes in a baobab link to holes in history and knowing.

(05) • Oil on Canvas

an oil study on flowers

Native South African Flora

My interest in flowers, particularly those native to South Africa and gathered from my family garden, stems from their ability to carry memory, place, and inheritance. These plants become quiet markers of personal and collective history, rooted within the landscapes that shaped both myself and the women before me. In preserving and working with them, I engage with heritage not as something monumental or fixed, but as something living, fragile, and deeply intimate. The garden becomes an archive of belonging, care, and continuity, where memory is held within the cycles of growth, decay, and return.

Archive & Lace

an Archive of Tender Things

2023 – 2026

“and i try to remember, to hold on”

These works explore preservation as both an act of care and an acknowledgment of inevitable loss. Through the use of pressed flowers and leaves stitched delicately into translucent pink and purple tulle, the pieces attempt to hold and archive fleeting moments before they disappear entirely. The organic materials, once living and vibrant, are suspended in a fragile state between life and decay, speaking to the transient nature of memory, identity, and time itself.

The translucent fabric functions almost like a veil, obscuring, softening, and distorting what lies beneath, much like memory does over time. Encased within wooden frames reminiscent of archival displays or domestic keepsakes, the works question what it means to preserve something inherently temporary. They become quiet meditations on tenderness, remembrance, and the human desire to hold onto what cannot remain unchanged.
